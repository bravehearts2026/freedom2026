Reason From the Goal,
Not From Tradition

A way of thinking Elon Musk used to build rockets ten times cheaper than anyone thought possible — and a habit you can use on almost anything. It's called first-principles thinking, and it's simpler than it sounds.

01

Two Ways to Solve a Problem

Most people only use one of these

Most of the time, when we solve a problem, we copy what's already been done. That's called reasoning by analogy — and it's usually fine, because most problems have already been solved by someone. But sometimes it quietly limits you to whatever the "normal" answer already is.

The usual way

Reasoning by Analogy

"This is how it's always been done, so this is roughly what it should cost / how long it should take / how hard it should be." You compare to what already exists and adjust a little. Fast, safe, and usually good enough — but it can't beat what already exists, because it's built from what already exists.

The Musk way

First-Principles Thinking

Break the problem down to the most basic true facts you're sure of — ignore how it's normally done — then build back up from those facts toward your actual goal. Slower to start. Sometimes reveals that the "normal" way was wildly inefficient.

02

The Story: Why SpaceX Exists

The example Musk tells most often

A $65 million quote, and a question nobody else was asking

In the early 2000s, Musk wanted to send something to Mars — partly just to get the public excited about space again. He went shopping for a rocket. The going price: as high as $65 million. He even flew to Russia to see about buying a used intercontinental ballistic missile instead. Still $8–20 million. Too expensive.

Almost everyone in that position would reason by analogy: "Rockets are expensive. That's just what they cost. Find the cheapest one." Musk asked a different question instead:

"What is a rocket actually made of?"

Broken down to its raw materials, a rocket is mostly:

Aerospace-grade aluminum Titanium Copper Carbon fiber

He looked up what those raw materials actually cost on the commodity market — the honest, no-markup price of the metal itself.

~2% of a typical rocket's selling price was the actual cost of what it's made of

That gap — somewhere between $60 million and the true material cost — wasn't really "the cost of building a rocket." It was mostly the cost of an industry that outsourced to subcontractors, who outsourced to their own subcontractors, with everyone adding a markup and nobody responsible for the whole thing. Musk's conclusion wasn't "rockets are just expensive." It was: the manufacturing process must be the broken part. So instead of buying a rocket, he started a company to build one, from the raw materials up. That company was SpaceX, and inside a few years it had cut the cost of a launch by roughly 10x — which is the entire reason SpaceX can now launch almost every single day, and the entire reason that other page on this site has a chart with 167 launches in one year.

03

He Did It Again With Batteries

Same method, different industry — Tesla

Years later at Tesla, people told Musk the same kind of thing: "Battery packs are expensive, and that's just the way they'll always be. Historically it's cost about $600 per kilowatt-hour." Same trap — reasoning by analogy, treating the current price as a law of nature.

Same question, applied again: what is a battery actually made of? Cobalt, nickel, aluminum, carbon, some polymers, a steel can. Priced on the London Metal Exchange, those raw materials came to roughly $80 per kilowatt-hour — not $600.

The $520 gap wasn't a law of physics. It was a manufacturing and supply-chain problem — which meant it was solvable. Tesla built its own battery production instead of accepting the "historical" price, and battery costs have fallen dramatically industry-wide since.

04

How to Actually Do This

Four steps, every time
1

Name the real goal — not the usual method

Musk's goal was never "buy a rocket." It was "get to Mars for less than $65 million." Separating the goal from the conventional way of reaching it is the whole trick.

2

Ask "why does it cost / take this much?" until you hit bedrock

Keep asking why until you reach something you're actually certain is true — a physical fact, a real constraint — not just "because that's how it's done" or "because that's what it's always cost."

3

Ignore the "normal" price or method completely

Once you know the true floor — the raw materials, the real minimum — forget what convention says it should cost. The gap between the floor and the "normal" price is where the opportunity is.

4

Build back up toward the goal, not toward the tradition

Design the solution from those fundamentals, aimed at what you actually need — even if it means doing the whole thing differently than everyone else does.

05

Try It On Something Small

This works on ordinary problems too

You don't need a rocket company to use this. It works on homework, on chores, on a sport you're stuck on, on saving money — anything where you've been told "that's just how it is."

Next time something feels stuck

Ask yourself these, in order

What am I actually trying to achieve here — not the method I've been using, the actual goal?

What do I know for certain is true, with no assumptions?

If I forgot how everyone else does this, what would I try?

That's it. Most of the time you'll still end up doing things the normal way — and that's fine, the normal way is often normal because it works. But every so often, this is how you find the 2%.
